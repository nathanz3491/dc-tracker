HOW US DATA CENTER PROJECTS ACTUALLY WORK

This section is background. Read it before the task below it.

HOW TO USE IT — this matters more than anything in it:

  * It is here so you can tell what a sentence MEANS and whether a reading is
    PLAUSIBLE. It is not a source of values.
  * Never output a number, a date, a name or a status that came from this
    section. If the document in front of you does not state it, the answer is
    still null.
  * Where this section and the document disagree, THE DOCUMENT WINS. Report what
    the document says. This section only tells you when to look twice.
  * Its job is to stop you accepting an impossible reading, not to let you supply
    a missing one.


1. WHAT A CAMPUS IS MADE OF

A data center project nests, and the levels get confused constantly:

  campus / site      one location, one operator. 100 acres to 4,000+ acres.
                     The thing this database tracks as a project.
  building / phase   one structure, or one delivery tranche of the campus.
                     Typically 50-300 MW at current AI scale. Named "Phase 1",
                     "Building A", "AZP-2", "Fairwater 2".
  data hall          a room inside a building. 5-50 MW. Rarely reported.

Floor area tracks capacity only loosely: 1-3 million sq ft is a typical
multi-hundred-MW campus, and 4+ million sq ft is among the largest built. Acreage
is looser still, because most of a site is setback, substation and laydown.


2. THE TWO DIFFERENT MEGAWATT NUMBERS — THE SINGLE COMMONEST ERROR

Articles about one project routinely quote capacity figures that are about
DIFFERENT THINGS, in the same paragraph, without saying so.

  IT / compute load     what the data center consumes. THIS is a data center's
                        capacity, and the only kind this database means by
                        "MW planned" or "MW built".
  facility load         IT load times PUE (1.1-1.3 at modern hyperscale sites).
  generating capacity   power plants — gas turbines, solar, nuclear uprates,
                        batteries — built to SERVE the site. Usually built and
                        owned by the ELECTRIC UTILITY, not by the data center
                        operator. It is NOT the data center's capacity.
  transmission          lines and substations. Rated in kV and MVA, not a
                        capacity of the data center at all.

Generation quoted for a site is normally LARGER than the load it serves, and by
different multiples depending on type:

  gas (CCGT)   roughly 1.1-1.5x the load, for reserve margin and outages
  solar        roughly 3-4x the load it is said to "match", because it produces
               about a quarter of nameplate over a year. A pledge to "match 100%
               of electricity use with renewables" is an ACCOUNTING claim about
               annual megawatt-HOURS. It is not capacity, and not for this site.

So a campus described as 2 GW may legitimately appear in the same article beside
2,262 MW of new gas plants and 1,500 MW of solar. Those three numbers are not
three tranches of a data center. Adding them is always wrong. Only the first is
the project's capacity.

Signals that a megawatt figure is GENERATION, not data center load: the subject
of the sentence is a utility (Entergy, Georgia Power, AEP, Dominion, Oncor); the
words plant, unit, turbine, CCGT, CCCT, generating, solar, wind, battery, BESS,
storage, nuclear, uprate, substation, transmission, interconnection, PPA; a
regulatory venue (a public service or utility commission, an IRP, a rate case);
or a phrase like "to power", "to serve", "to supply".


3. WHAT THE MONEY FIGURES ARE

Investment figures are quoted at wildly different scopes, and the largest number
in an article is very often not the one about this site:

  site capex           building this campus. What this database wants.
  programme total      a company's whole multi-site build ("the $500B Stargate
                       programme"). Not this site.
  regional impact      what a state announces as investment "in the region" or
                       "in the state". Usually broader than the site.
  utility capex        what the utility spends on plants and lines to serve it.
                       A different party's balance sheet.
  financing structures JV commitments, SPV bond issues, debt raised, lease
                       obligations, residual value guarantees. These describe how
                       a project is paid for, and they overlap the capex rather
                       than adding to it. $27B of debt into an SPV for a campus
                       whose cost is $27B is ONE $27B, not two.
  chip / IT spend      servers and accelerators, often several times the shell
                       cost, sometimes quoted as an all-in total.

Never add two money figures together. If two are stated, they are far more likely
to be the same money described twice, or two different scopes, than two costs
that sum.


4. THE PLAUSIBILITY ENVELOPE

Orders of magnitude, for noticing that a reading cannot be right. Not for
computing values, and not a reason to overrule a document.

  Campus IT load       most announced US campuses are 100 MW - 1 GW.
                       Above 2 GW is exceptional and newsworthy.
                       ~5 GW is, as of 2026, the largest announced ANYWHERE.
                       A single campus above 6 GW almost certainly means several
                       different things have been added together.
  Facility capex       roughly $8-15M per MW of IT load, i.e. $10-15B per GW for
                       the buildings and power infrastructure. Including
                       accelerators, $30-50B per GW is normal for an AI campus.
                       So: a multi-GW AI campus reported at $10B is very likely a
                       SUPERSEDED or PARTIAL figure. Under ~$3M per MW, or over
                       ~$50M per MW, something has been mismatched.
  Land                 roughly 1,000-3,000 acres for a GW-scale campus.
  Timeline             announcement to first power is typically 3-5 years, and
                       full buildout of a multi-GW campus 5-10. A campus
                       announced in the last two years is not operational at
                       scale, whatever a headline implies.
  Jobs                 hundreds of permanent operations staff even at GW scale;
                       thousands of CONSTRUCTION workers. A large construction
                       headcount is evidence of construction, never of operation.


5. WHO THE PARTIES ARE

Several companies appear in every article, and only one of them is the operator:

  operator / owner     the hyperscaler or developer: Meta, Microsoft, Amazon,
                       Google, OpenAI, xAI, Oracle, QTS, Vantage, Crusoe, Switch.
  end customer         the tenant, when it differs from the operator.
  electric utility     Entergy, Georgia Power, AEP, Dominion, Oncor, WEC. Builds
                       generation and transmission. NEVER the operator.
  contractors / EPC    Turner, DPR, Mortenson, Holder, Clayco, Fluor, Bechtel.
                       They build it. They do not own or operate it.
  financial partners   Blue Owl, Brookfield, KKR, and project SPVs with names
                       unrelated to the campus. They fund it.
  government bodies    a public service commission, a parish or county council,
                       a state economic development agency. They approve, tax and
                       announce it.

An operator often holds a minority stake in the JV that owns its own campus. That
is a financing structure; the operator is still the operator.


6. THE LIFECYCLE, AND WHAT THE WORDS ACTUALLY MEAN

The usual order, though real projects run these in parallel:

  announced -> land acquired -> permits filed -> permits approved ->
  interconnection secured -> site work -> groundbreaking -> vertical construction
  -> equipment installed -> energized -> commissioned -> serving a customer

Phrases that are constantly misread as meaning the site is RUNNING, and do not:

  "broke ground", "since breaking ground"        construction started
  "under construction", "crews on site"          construction
  "has contracted $X with local businesses"      construction spending
  "peak construction workforce of N"             a construction FORECAST
  "once operational, it will support N jobs"     explicitly NOT yet operational
  "expected online", "targeted for", "scheduled" a plan, not an event
  "is being built", "will be the largest"        future tense

A site is operational only when the article says power is flowing and the
facility is in service or serving a customer. Nothing else counts. Note also that
a utility energizing a SUBSTATION, or bringing its own generating plant online,
is not the data center coming online.


7. FIGURES GET RESTATED — LATER SUPERSEDES EARLIER

These projects are announced small and repeatedly enlarged. One campus commonly
has three or four different capacities and investment figures in circulation, all
correctly reported at the time. A real and typical sequence for one site:

  Dec 2024   $10B, 2 GW
  Oct 2025   $27B via a joint venture
  Jul 2026   more than $50B, 5 GW

These are not conflicting claims. They are one project restated as it grew, and
the CURRENT figure is the latest one. An older article is not less reliable — it
is reporting a scope that has since changed. When you can see the date a figure
was stated, or a phrase like "up from", "now", "expanded to", "originally",
"initially", "doubling", that is the most important thing about it.

The mirror image also happens: capacity is cut, phases are deferred, projects are
paused or cancelled. Later still wins.


8. WHAT ACTUALLY OBSTRUCTS THESE PROJECTS

In rough order of how often they bind, for calibrating whether a reported concern
is a real obstacle to THIS project:

  power       interconnection queues run 4-7 years in constrained markets; new
              generation and transmission lag load by years. The defining
              bottleneck of this cycle, and the reason a finished building can
              sit empty.
  permits     local zoning, special exceptions, air permits for backup or onsite
              generation, and organized community opposition over noise, water,
              traffic, tax abatements and who pays for the power.
  equipment   transformers and switchgear on multi-year lead times; large gas
              turbines are sold out years ahead.
  water       cooling water supply and discharge, especially in the arid west.
  commercial  financing, and whether a tenant is committed.

Being expensive, large, or controversial is not by itself an obstacle. A concern
becomes an obstacle when someone with standing — a regulator, a court, a utility,
an organized group — can stop or delay the work, and the document says so.
